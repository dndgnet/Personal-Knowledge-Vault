# What Should Be Included in a Good Project Description?

A good project description (sometimes called a project summary, project charter narrative, or project overview) is a concise yet complete statement that gives stakeholders a shared understanding of *what* is being done, *why* it matters, and *how* success will be judged. It is typically one of the first artifacts created and is referenced throughout the project lifecycle.

Below is a practical structure that aligns with PMI standards.

 
## 1. Business Problem / Opportunity Statement
- Describe the current pain or opportunity in business terms (not technical jargon).
- Quantify the impact where possible (time wasted, error rate, cost, compliance risk, revenue opportunity).
- Answer: “Why are we doing this now?”

## 2. Project Purpose / Goal
- One or two sentences stating the high-level outcome the project will achieve.
- Focus on the *business result*, not the solution.
- Example: “Reduce average customer onboarding time from 12 days to under 3 days while improving data accuracy and auditability.”

## 3. Objectives (SMART)
- 3–6 specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound objectives.
- These become the basis for success criteria and benefits tracking.
- Prefer outcome metrics over activity metrics.

## 4. Scope Statement
- **In scope**: Major deliverables, processes, systems, user groups, and geographies included.
- **Out of scope**: Explicitly list what is *not* included to prevent scope creep.
- High-level only — detailed scope belongs in the scope baseline or WBS.

## 5. Key Deliverables
- Tangible outputs the project will produce (e.g., Power App, data model, automated flow, training materials, cutover plan, support model).
- Keep the list short and focused on what the business will actually receive.

## 6. Success Criteria / Definition of Done
- How will we know the project succeeded?
- Include both delivery criteria (accepted, deployed, trained) and outcome criteria (KPIs improved, benefits realized).
- Reference any required gates (security review, architecture review, UAT sign-off, hypercare completion).

## 7. High-Level Approach / Solution Outline
- Brief description of the chosen approach or solution direction (without diving into design).
- Mention key technologies only if they are already decided or constrained (e.g., “Leverage existing Power Platform environment and Dataverse”).
- Note any major constraints, assumptions, or dependencies.

## 8. Stakeholders & Governance
- Primary sponsor / product owner.
- Key stakeholder groups (and their interest).
- Decision-making body or steering committee (if known).
- RACI at a very high level if helpful.

## 9. Timeline & Major Milestones (High-Level)
- Target start and end dates or overall duration.
- 3–5 major milestones or phases (e.g., Discovery complete, MVP ready for UAT, Go-Live, Benefits review).
- Avoid detailed schedules here.

## 10. Budget / Resource Envelope (if known)
- Order-of-magnitude cost or funding source.
- Core team composition or key skill needs.
- Any major external costs (licenses, vendors, infrastructure).

## 11. Risks, Assumptions, Constraints & Dependencies (Summary)
- Top 3–5 items only. Full registers live elsewhere.
- Especially important: organizational change, data quality, integration points, regulatory requirements, and resource availability.

## 12. Benefits & Value Proposition
- Expected tangible and intangible benefits.
- Link back to strategic goals or OKRs where possible.
- Note how benefits will be measured and when they are expected to start accruing.

## 13. Related Documents & References
- Link to business case, previous discovery, architecture decision records, similar projects, or strategic roadmap items.

---

## Recommended Length and Style
- **Ideal length**: 1–2 pages (or a well-structured single SharePoint/Confluence page / PowerPoint slide + appendix).
- Write in plain business language first; technical details can be linked.
- Use active voice and present/future tense.
- Make it scannable: short paragraphs, bullet points, bold key phrases, and clear headings.
- Version-control it and treat it as a living document that is baselined at major gates.

## Extra Tips for IT / Power Platform / Data Projects
- Explicitly call out data sources, security/compliance classification, and ALM/DevOps approach at a high level.
- Note whether the solution is citizen-developer, pro-developer, or hybrid.
- Clarify support model after go-live (who owns it — BAU team, CoE, vendor, etc.).
- If the project is part of a larger program or roadmap, show the relationship.

## Common Anti-Patterns to Avoid
- Turning the description into a full requirements document or technical design.
- Focusing only on activities (“we will build X, Y, Z”) instead of outcomes.
- Omitting out-of-scope items.
- Using internal jargon or acronyms without explanation.
- Making it so long that no one reads it.

---

A strong project description becomes the “north star” for the team and the primary communication tool for sponsors and stakeholders. When written well, it dramatically reduces misalignment, accelerates onboarding of new team members, and makes status reporting and change control much easier.
